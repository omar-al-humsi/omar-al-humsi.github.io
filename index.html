Research

Job Market Paper

Firm Shocks and Worker Trajectories: Evidence from Linked Employer–Employee Data

Sole author

Paper (PDF) Slides Code

Abstract This paper studies how idiosyncratic firm-level shocks transmit to worker earnings, mobility, and long-run career trajectories. Using administrative linked employer–employee data and a novel identification strategy based on exogenous variation in firm export demand, I show that negative shocks generate persistent earnings losses concentrated among workers with the least transferable skills. A structural decomposition attributes roughly two-thirds of these losses to reduced within-firm wage growth rather than displacement. The results imply that policies targeting retention during transitory shocks can substantially reduce long-run earnings scarring.

Other Working Papers

Teacher Turnover and Student Achievement: Quasi-Experimental Evidence

with Co-author One and Co-author Two

Paper (PDF) Slides Code

Abstract We estimate the causal effect of teacher turnover on student outcomes by exploiting staggered pension-eligibility thresholds that generate plausibly exogenous variation in retirement timing. Turnover reduces test scores in affected classrooms, with the effect driven almost entirely by the loss of high value-added teachers. Replacement quality, not disruption per se, explains the bulk of the achievement decline.

Minimum Wages and the Composition of Job Vacancies

with Co-author Three

Paper (PDF) Slides Code

Abstract Using the near-universe of online job postings, we document how minimum wage increases reshape the composition of advertised vacancies. Firms respond by raising posted skill requirements and consolidating part-time positions into full-time roles, consistent with labor–labor substitution toward more productive workers rather than aggregate employment reductions.

Information Frictions in Graduate Program Choice

Sole author

Paper (PDF) Slides Code

Abstract A randomized information intervention reveals that prospective graduate students systematically misperceive program-level labor market returns. Correcting these beliefs shifts application portfolios toward higher-return programs, with the largest responses among first-generation applicants, suggesting information frictions contribute to socioeconomic sorting in graduate education.
