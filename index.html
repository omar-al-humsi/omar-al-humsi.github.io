Job Market Paper

Labor Taxes as Investment Policy:
Optimal Income Taxation with Collective Wage Bargaining

This paper studies optimal redistributive income taxation when firms invest before wages are collectively bargained and capital cannot be taxed or subsidized directly. Extending an extensive-margin model of unionized labor markets to endogenous investment, I show that optimal participation taxes (the income tax paid when working plus the unemployment benefit forgone) can exceed those in the fixed-capital benchmark. At a given capital stock, higher participation taxes raise negotiated wages and lower employment, reducing the return to additional capital that the firm retains. But by making employment more responsive to capital, they can also lower capital-to-wage pass-through, the wage increase the firm anticipates from additional capital. Investment rises when this effect dominates. The optimal participation tax formula is therefore augmented by an investment effect: the investment response valued at the marginal social value of capital. Because capital-induced wage gains are paid out of the output that capital adds rather than redistributed from firm owners, they enter this value at workers’ marginal social welfare weights. When higher participation taxes crowd in socially valuable capital, the resulting welfare gain partially offsets the efficiency costs of income redistribution, which unions raise through involuntary unemployment; when large enough, it can justify taxing participation even for workers with above-average welfare weights. In a numerical calibration to France, letting firms anticipate that capital raises negotiated wages (investment hold-up) increases the average optimal participation tax rate by 4.92 percentage points, leaving it only 0.48 percentage points below the perfectly competitive benchmark.

Other Research

Directed Technical Change and Optimal Participation Taxes in Imperfect Labor Markets

Working paper · Presented at the LMU Munich Public Economics Seminar, 2026

When the direction of technical change responds to the skill composition of employment, taxes on work also steer innovation. I study optimal participation taxes in an extensive-margin model where wages are set through sectoral collective bargaining, which rations jobs, or by firms with monopsony power, which mark them down. Redistributive income taxes then change employment by skill group, and hence the direction of innovation, through wage setting as well as participation decisions. In the competitive benchmark, discouraging low-skilled employment raises their wage at given technology but redirects innovation toward technologies that complement skilled labor, which lowers it. Directed technical change thus weakens, and under strong equilibrium bias reverses, the case for compressing pre-tax wages by taxing low-skilled participation, strengthening the case for participation subsidies.
